# Script voice: Josh's rules for Lonvera ad scripts (2026-11-18)

Learned rewriting the 30 Madre briefs for the week of 11/23 (working folder `~/Desktop/Google Sheets/lonvera_creative_key/cycles/c2026-11-18-madre-rewrites/`; WRITER_BRIEF.md and GRADER_BRIEF.md there are the full working versions). Applies to every Lonvera script, hook and VO brief.

## What Josh rejected in the original briefs
- Generic words that mean nothing, no empathy, no specific customer: "Daily nutrition is one place to start", "You want a clear conversation about what to do next", "Keep your regular meals in the picture", "Give your daily nutrition a closer look".
- One body script per product with only the first 5-20 seconds changed.
- Mechanism as a textbook nutrient list ("B12 supports energy. Iron carries oxygen. Copper helps your body use iron.").
- No proof, no outcome, no offer in Black Friday week.
- His verdict on a first rewrite (VB-AI-08 roofer): "totally awful... I have no idea what he is even saying... a ton of words that mean nothing." Trade jargon ("seven-twelve pitch", "first shingle"), metaphors ("whips a tired horse"), fragments and nutrient words crammed into 150 words.

## The model Josh called "amazing" (VB-AI-03, with his own edits)
> Her hand's on my back. I'm breathing slow so she thinks I'm asleep. The last two times didn't go well. So now I fake sleep. Thirty-one years married, and I'm hiding pills in my truck console like a teenager. The blue pill forces it for four hours, then you're back to feeling insufficient. It feeds nothing. The glands that make you want her don't seem to be working these days. Vital Bull seems to make them work. It's kinda crazy that it actually works. ...
First person, present tense, one private moment anyone instantly understands, short plain sentences that follow each other, real shame, one-line contrast mechanism (the pill forces it / this makes them work), proof, offer.

## Josh's own line edits (the taste to match)
| Was | Josh's version | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| "then hands you back the same man" | "then you're back to feeling insufficient" | plain honest feeling beats clever phrasing |
| "The glands that make you want her are starving" | "The glands that make you want her don't seem to be working these days" | everyday words, no drama |
| "Vital Bull feeds them." | "Vital Bull seems to make them work. It's kinda crazy that it actually works." | results told with mild disbelief, no product-copy verbs (feeds, fuels, restores) |
| "Beef liver for B12 and heme iron" | "Beef liver HAS B12 and heme iron" | say what the food has; never "X for Y" shorthand |
| "My grandfather ate liver and heart every week" | "they ate simple whole foods" | nobody claims their parents/grandparents ate organs |
| "What I eat all day is coffee and a sandwich at my desk" | "I have pretty much no nutrients all day. Just coffee and a simple sandwich. Pretty much no whole foods for things like iron, which apparently you need for energy." | state the gap plainly; learned facts come out as "apparently" |
| "This year I looked it up. The organs I leave behind have more vitamins than the steaks I carry out." | "Every November, I shoot an elk, carry out the tasty meat, and leave the liver and heart for the rats. My hunting buddy gave me shit this year because apparently the organs I leave behind have more vitamins and minerals than all the steaks I carry out." | people learn things from someone who ribbed them; casual, mild swearing fine |
| "Those fifteen minutes are for my hands and knees." | "Those fifteen minutes are so I can smack around my hands and knees to get some feeling into them." | physical, blunt, a little funny |
| "I'm forty-eight, and I already look it." | "I'm forty-eight, but apparently I look seventy." | say the gap plainly and a little funny; "apparently" again carries what someone else thinks |
| "My hair's thinning, so I clean teeth all day with it clipped up so patients can't see." | "My hair is thinning, so I keep it clipped up all day, since I'm embarrassed my patients will see." | say the feeling (embarrassed) out loud; the job shows in the visual, not a clause |
Josh's lines are ground truth: lock them; never let a writer or grader "fix" them.

## Format variety (Josh: "They need to be a variety of format")
A round must mix formats; 29 of 30 first drafts were the same first-person voiceover over dramatized scenes. Josh: "yappers, historical videos, animations, podcast style, any format really... the POV of a hair follicle on someone's head talking to them... come up with unique formats." Give every script its own format, no two alike in a round. Bolder ideas used in the Madre 11/23 round: object POV (tailor's tape measure), body-part POV (a knee, a hair follicle talking to its owner), yapper rant in a truck, sports-announcer commentary, wife's text thread on screen, podcast mics, kid interviewing grandma, cartoon organs arguing, stop-motion calendar, historical newsreel, document read-aloud (annual review), running-app diary screens, kitchen science experiment. Baseline formats: UGC selfie talking head; cinematic first-person voiceover; someone else tells it (spouse, kid, friend); two-person dialogue skit; interview (off-camera questions); POV text-on-screen with minimal voiceover; expert-in-their-world explainer with a demo; tabletop side-by-side teardown; 3D mechanism animation; diary/vlog day entries; reply-to-comment / green-screen reaction. Write the script for its format (who speaks, on camera or not, what we see).

## Josh's edits teach a voice, not a template
Every line Josh fixed got copied into every script by the writers ("kinda crazy that it works" 8 of 10, "that's how I ended up on Lonvera" 9 of 10, "I figured it was" 10 of 10, a buddy's put-down 8 of 10, one close skeleton 10 of 10) and scores dropped. No phrase, beat or story shape in more than 2 scripts of a product (3 across a round). Freeze scripts that pass and fix only their flagged lines; full rewrites each round lose what worked.

## Rules
1. **One-listen clarity (overrides everything).** A stranger understands every sentence once, at normal speed. No jargon, no metaphors, no fragments, no numbers without context (say "normal starts at twelve"), no nutrient lists. Up to 250 spoken words (Josh 2026-11-19; was 100-130): use the room for the bridge, mechanism and proof, never filler.
1b. **A clear progression, one thought leading to the next** (Josh on BF-AI-06: the original jumped from turmeric to his job to the swelling to "the other half was the worn-out padding" and he called it "not a clear progression from one thought to the next"). Tell it in story order: who I am and when it hurts -> what I thought it was -> what I tried -> what happened -> what I learned and from whom -> what I tried instead and why it's different -> what changed -> the offer. Never open with a detail whose meaning only arrives later. Never jump straight to the product: first say what the person wanted next, then how they got to the product (Josh on VB-AI-10: the clinic opening was "great" but "jumps right into Vital Bull without explaining that they want a way to make it on their own... and that's how they ended up on Vital Bull" -> "I didn't want something that takes over. I wanted my body to start making its own again, and that's how I ended up on Vital Bull."). Every line must match what the line before set up (Josh on BL-AI-04: "fourteen pills" followed by "Bottom tier: energy chews" -> "says pills then the first one is a chew?"). Time phrases in plain speech: "after four months", not "at month four".
1d. **Every detail says why it matters** (Josh 2026-11-19 on BL-AI-01: "'I count pills for work'... there is no context to that... like MAYBE 'since I'm a pharmacist who gives out prescriptions for a living, I can't be moving slow and getting these things wrong'"). A job, a number, a place or a habit is only in the script if the same sentence ties it to the stakes: what goes wrong for this person because of the problem. Odd job wording fails even with the stakes attached (Josh 2026-11-19: "I count patients' pills for a living, so one slip means someone gets the wrong dose" -> "'I count pills for a living' feels weird"); name the job the way people say it: "I work in a hospital pharmacy filling prescriptions. I can't be moving slow and getting these things wrong." Bare labels ("I count pills for work", "I run half-marathons", "I'm a bank teller") fail, and so do details the viewer has to decode (Josh on BF-AI-02: "My knees can't do my daughter's twenty-two steps anymore... I take Aleve at eight and eight": "I have no idea what the daughter's twenty-two steps are"; say "the steep stairs up to my daughter's front door", "every morning and every night"); Odd specifics that only fit one person's life read as trivia, not empathy (Josh on VB-AI-05: "At forty-nine, I can't carry water heaters anymore. My helper at my Fort Worth plumbing company carries them now." -> "this oddly specific fact feels out of place"): say the feeling the viewer shares ("At forty-nine, I'm not as strong as I used to be. I'm a plumber, and my young helper does the heavy lifting now.") and leave the specific object, city or brand in the visual. Insider places and trade terms count too (Josh on BF-AI-06: "My buddy at the supply house" -> "Supply house? who the hell says that?"): "my buddy from work". Same for "I'm living on energy drinks to get through Phoenix roofs in the heat" (Josh: "To get through Phoenix roofs in the heat?") -> "I'm living on energy drinks just to make it through a workday", and for an unnamed document like "My review said I seem checked out" (Josh: "be more explicit about 'my review', I'm not sure what that is") -> "My boss wrote 'seems checked out' on my yearly job review." "I fill prescriptions all day, so moving slow means getting someone's meds wrong" passes.
1f. **One number per sentence, said the way a person says it** (Josh 2026-11-19 on BL-AI-04: "I'm still tired on fourteen pills from eleven bottles. That's a hundred forty a month" -> "I'm not sure what 'on fourteen pills from eleven bottles' means... and what that has to do with being tired?"). No stacked numbers, no "on X" / "X from Y" shorthand; tie the number to the problem: "I take fourteen vitamins a day, and I'm still so tired I lean on the grocery cart. That's a hundred forty dollars a month on pills that don't work."
1e. **Say why a nutrient or test is relevant before using it** (Josh 2026-11-19 on BL-AI-03: "I'm walking the hill I used to run, and my doctor says my iron is normal" is "a strong hook... but assumes the viewer knows why we are talking about iron... the viewer might not know iron is low in runners"). Never name a nutrient, hormone, test or body part before the viewer knows how it connects to the problem; add the link in one plain clause ("iron is what carries oxygen to your legs, and runners lose it every mile"; "testosterone, the hormone behind your drive"; "collagen, what the cushion in your knee is made of").
1c. **Guide a cold viewer to the solution; never assume they know or care about organs** (Josh 2026-11-19, second time this came up, after VB-AI-10: "you're assuming these cold audiences know about beef liver, or care about beef liver... and just jump into 'beef liver has XYZ'... you need to guide them to beef liver as the solution"). His example of the failure: "I drank spinach smoothies... Nothing changed. Then I read what I'd tell a patient to read. The iron in liver comes with the copper and vitamin A your body needs to use it." Liver had never been mentioned, so the line lands as a non sequitur. Every script must walk these bridge steps in plain words before any ingredient claim: (a) why what they tried didn't work, in one plain sentence; (b) what they learned was actually missing, and from whom (a person or a moment, not "I read"); (c) where that thing is found, introduced as a food first ("red meat, especially liver"), so the organ arrives as the answer to a question the viewer already has; (d) the natural objection said out loud ("I'm not eating liver"); (e) how they got to the product (who told them, what it is in plain words: "dried beef liver in a capsule"); only then (f) why it's different, then the result. The organ gets its own sentence, answering a question the character just asked; never tacked onto the end of another claim (Josh on BL-AI-01: "The iron your body keeps comes from meat, and liver has about three times what a steak does. I can't even look at liver." -> "throwing liver out with no context... just like in the middle of a sentence"; fixed: "I asked what does work. He said the iron your body keeps is the kind in red meat, and one of the richest sources is beef liver, the organ itself, with about three times the iron of a steak."). Test: delete everything after the product's first mention; the part before it must already make the viewer want that thing.
2. **One very specific, unique customer per script.** Age, situation, what they tried and why it failed, the private sentence they say to themselves, written in their words. No two scripts in a round share a customer, story, name, setting or payoff (watch spouse payoffs, 2 a.m. wake-ups, "tired by afternoon").
3. **Real-person language, never marketing speak.** Banned: "3 PM crash", "afternoon slump", "brain fog", "hit a wall", "running on empty", "out of gas", "feel like yourself again", "like I was 18/25 again", "I stopped reaching for the bottle", "the bottle stayed on the shelf", "she asked what I'd been doing / I just smiled", "your wife notices", "it's not your fault", "sound familiar?", "here's the thing", "the truth is", "game-changer", "unlock", "fuel your", "turn back the clock", "say goodbye to", "finally", "no more", "imagine if", "you're not alone", "they weren't tougher, they were fed better", "starving for nutrients", "the missing piece", "nature's", "ancestral secret", "doctors won't tell you", "nobody told me". Check how people actually talk (Reddit, real reviews).
4. **Mechanism = a reason, not a list**, in one or two plain sentences, best as a contrast with what that person already tried (the pill forces it vs this makes them work; herbs push vs giving the body the material; turmeric only fixed the swelling half). Different wording in every script.
5. **Proof: two types**, varied across scripts: a named customer quote with age and a concrete detail (finished text, Josh's claims pass reviews it), what a spouse/buddy/coworker/doctor said, a number the person checked, a before/after moment. Never the same quote twice; no invented clinical figures or customer counts.
6. **Offer + deadline + guarantee, worded differently in every script**, tied to that customer (e.g. "gets you to your own retest"). Say the regular price out loud.
7. **Disbelief beats sparingly.** Josh's "kinda crazy that it actually works" is a voice, not a template: at most 2-3 per round, never a tacked-on fragment.
8. **Visuals show the moment or the mechanism and never claim what the voiceover does not.**
9. No compliance hedging (execution rule 11).

10. **Selling angle Josh approved (2026-11-19): whole food, not synthetic.** These are nutrient-dense whole foods, not the synthetic, lab-made vitamins in most pills and fortified foods. Use it where it explains why the old pill failed, in plain words ("it's real food, not a lab-made vitamin"), varied, never as "nature's", "ancestral secret" or an attack on doctors. Sell harder without spam: one real deadline, no hype words, no fake scarcity, selling said the way a friend recommends something.

11. **Organ-meat nutrient facts (Josh 2026-11-19: "all the nutrients facts in organ meat").** Usable, per equal weight vs steak (USDA FoodData Central, raw): liver has about 40x the B12, about 100x the copper, about 3x the iron (heme iron, the kind the body takes in best), and is one of the richest sources of vitamin A, folate and choline, which steak barely has. Versus fruits and vegetables too (Josh: "don't just compare to steak, compare to fruits and veggies as well"): no fruit or vegetable has any B12; liver has about 6x the vitamin A of carrots (ready-to-use form), about 2x the iron of spinach (heme, absorbed far better), over 100x the copper of kale or spinach, and more folate than spinach; never compare vitamin C or fiber. Say them about the FOOD ("ounce for ounce, liver has..."), never about a capsule or dose; one fact per script, as a comparison a person would repeat to a friend; never a nutrient list; no numbers for testicle, prostate, marrow or collagen until verified. All go to Josh's claims pass.

12. **Testosterone decline (Vita Bull; Josh 2026-11-19).** Verified: men's average testosterone fell about 1% a year beyond normal aging (Massachusetts Male Aging Study, Travison 2007, 1987-2004), and the drop shows in young US men too (Lokeshwar 2020, 1999-2016). Say it plainly ("men today have lower testosterone than men the same age did thirty years ago"). Never "60% in 30 years" (the live Vital Bull page overstates it) and no microplastics / seed oils / soy / doctor blame. One mention per script, where it answers "why is this happening to me".

13. **Zinc claims (2026-11-19 correction).** True: "your body needs zinc to make testosterone"; "beef is one of the best foods for zinc". False or unverified, never say: organs/testicles have the most zinc of any food (oysters and beef muscle are higher), "testosterone is made from zinc" (made from cholesterol). Don't lean every Vita Bull script on one mechanism.

## Process that worked
Writers per product (session model) -> an independent grader that did not write them, scoring the RMBC ad-creative-audit (`reference/rmbc/ad-creative-audit.md`, framework `reference/rmbc/rmbc-context.md`, copied from github.com/coleschaffer/copywritingskills-rmbc 2026-11-18; hook, mechanism, proof, CTA, visual-copy, 20 each; pass 80) plus the one-listen clarity test and the banned list -> revise below 80 -> regrade. Writer self-scores ran 10-20 points above the independent grader; never trust self-scores. Show Josh samples early: his line edits taught more than any rubric.
